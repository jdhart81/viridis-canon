\begin{abstract}
We study a deliberately narrow accounting model for complementary learners.
Component $i$ supplies task share $w_i>0$, has fixed information-efficiency
$d_i>0$, receives power $p_i\geq0$, and obeys the conditional ceiling
$r_i\leq p_i d_i/\epsilon$, where $\epsilon>0$ is an energy-per-bit factor.
Under $\sum_iw_i=1$, shared budget $\sum_i p_i\leq P$, and conjunctive
requirements $r_i\geq w_iR$, the jointly usable rate satisfies
$R\leq P/[\epsilon\sum_i(w_i/d_i)]$. The bound is attained by allocating power
proportional to $w_i/d_i$ and equalizing normalized component rates. Its
effective coefficient is the weighted harmonic mean of the $d_i$ and cannot
exceed the best coefficient. For an additive objective, the separate ceiling
is $P\max_i d_i/\epsilon$. Thus complementarity can change task coverage and
allocation but, under fixed coefficients and total power, cannot manufacture raw
capacity. Sixteen preregistered checks support the algebra; novelty and
significance remain unassessed. The exact declared Lean statements are Comparator-certified.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} Intelligence Bound; symbiosis; complementarity; collective intelligence; bottleneck allocation; harmonic mean; thermodynamic accounting.

\section{Introduction}
Collective intelligence can improve when agents preserve different knowledge
and integrate it effectively. Current agent-based work reports that synergy and
complementarity predict group performance, while redundant or poorly integrated
knowledge slows solution discovery \cite{garg2026}. Ecological mutualisms likewise
depend on partner structure and niche complementarity rather than partnership as
an undifferentiated label \cite{cai2020}. These findings motivate an accounting
question: does complementarity create processing capacity, or does it change the
task served by a fixed capacity?

The distinction matters inside the Intelligence Bound program. Run 072 modeled
predictive-information exchange between coupled systems but retained a global
thermodynamic constraint. Here we freeze total power and every component's
efficiency, then ask what follows without a coupling subsidy. The answer is a
negative certificate. A complementary task is limited by a weighted harmonic
mean; partnership alone cannot exceed the best fixed coefficient. A measured
gain may still be real, but it must enter through more power, improved
coefficients, avoided duplication, information reuse or coding, or a changed
objective.

The mathematics is elementary and close to bottleneck allocation, relay
capacity, and network information flow \cite{cover1979,ahlswede2000}. We make no
priority claim. The contribution is a bounded claim-hygiene baseline for
symbiotic Intelligence-Bound arguments.

\section{Methods and assumptions}
There are $n\geq1$ required task components. Component $i$ carries normalized
task share $w_i>0$, with $\sum_i w_i=1$, and has a fixed coefficient $d_i>0$.
It receives power $p_i\geq0$ from a shared budget
\begin{equation}
  \sum_i p_i\leq P. \label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
Its usable information rate $r_i$ obeys
\begin{equation}
  r_i\leq \frac{p_i d_i}{\epsilon},\qquad \epsilon>0. \label{eq:component}
\end{equation}
For an ideal Landauer reading, $\epsilon=k_BT\ln2$, but the proofs need only a
positive conversion factor. A conjunctive task rate $R$ requires every component
at its declared share:
\begin{equation}
  r_i\geq w_iR\quad\text{for all }i. \label{eq:required}
\end{equation}

The coefficients, weights, and total power are fixed. Components are accounted
for separately: no bit satisfies two requirements, no coding creates reusable
combinations, and no partner changes another's $d_i$. There are no queues,
latencies, strategic effects, learning curves, or ecological population
dynamics. These restrictions define the theorem rather than approximate all
collective learning.

\section{Results}
\textbf{Theorem 1 (conjunctive rate ceiling).} Every feasible allocation obeys
\begin{equation}
  R\leq R^*:=\frac{P}{\epsilon\sum_i w_i/d_i}. \label{eq:main}
\end{equation}

\emph{Proof.} Equations \eqref{eq:component} and \eqref{eq:required} imply
$p_i\geq\epsilon w_iR/d_i$. Summing and applying Eq.~\eqref{eq:budget} gives
$P\geq\epsilon R\sum_i w_i/d_i$, which rearranges to Eq.~\eqref{eq:main}.
\hfill$\square$

\textbf{Theorem 2 (attainment).} The allocation
\begin{equation}
  p_i^*=P\frac{w_i/d_i}{\sum_j w_j/d_j} \label{eq:allocation}
\end{equation}
exhausts the budget and attains $R^*$. It equalizes
$r_i/w_i=R^*$ when each component runs at its ceiling.

\emph{Proof.} The allocation sums to $P$. Substitution into
$p_i^*d_i/\epsilon$ yields
$w_iP/[\epsilon\sum_j w_j/d_j]=w_iR^*$ for every $i$. \hfill$\square$

\textbf{Corollary 1 (no free capacity).} Define
\begin{equation}
  d_{\mathrm{eff}}=\left(\sum_i\frac{w_i}{d_i}\right)^{-1}.
\end{equation}
This is the weighted harmonic mean of the coefficients, so
$d_{\mathrm{eff}}\leq\max_i d_i$. Equality holds when all positive-weight
components share the same coefficient. Complementarity changes the required
coverage but does not lift the fixed-coefficient thermodynamic ceiling.

\textbf{Corollary 2 (two partners).} For two components,
\begin{equation}
 R^*=\frac{Pd_1d_2}{\epsilon(w_1d_2+w_2d_1)},\quad
 p_1^*=\frac{Pw_1d_2}{w_1d_2+w_2d_1}.
\end{equation}
With equal task shares, the lower-efficiency partner receives more power.

\textbf{Theorem 3 (additive objective).} If component bits are fungible and the
objective is $\sum_i r_i$, then
\begin{equation}
  \sum_i r_i\leq\frac{P\max_i d_i}{\epsilon}, \label{eq:additive}
\end{equation}
attained by allocating all power to a best coefficient. This solution is not a
symbiotic design; it is the correct control showing that the objective, not the
budget algebra, creates the need for division of labor.

A nonvacuity witness uses $P=12$, $\epsilon=2$, weights $(1/2,1/2)$, and
coefficients $(1/2,1)$. The optimal powers are $(8,4)$ and the jointly usable
rate is $R^*=4$, so each component supplies rate $2$.

\section{Predictions and tests}
Within the declared model, the certificate is refuted by any feasible
allocation above Eq.~\eqref{eq:main}, failure of Eq.~\eqref{eq:allocation} to
attain the bound, or a weighted harmonic coefficient above the largest
component coefficient. An empirical test should predeclare task shares, measure
per-component throughput under isolated power increments, hold total power
fixed, and compare equal power with Eq.~\eqref{eq:allocation}. A rate above the
baseline is evidence that at least one frozen assumption changed; it does not by
itself identify synergy, coding, reduced duplication, or coefficient uplift.

\section{Discussion and conclusion}
The certificate separates two claims often blended in discussions of
symbiosis. First, complementary partners may be necessary to cover different
parts of a task. Second, partnership may improve the physical conversion of
power into usable information. The first is encoded by the weights and
conjunctive objective. The second is absent because $d_i$ and $P$ are frozen.
Calling the first a capacity increase would double count task structure as new
thermodynamic headroom.

This negative result does not contradict empirical collective synergy. It makes
such synergy more informative: an observed gain requires a measurable route
through changed efficiency, new power, avoided duplication, reusable coding, or
another explicit mechanism. The appropriate route is working-corpus
significance review, not Canon or spine promotion.

\section{Limitations}
The component ceiling is assumed, not derived from microscopic physics. Fixed
$d_i$ excludes learning, mutual prediction, and niche construction. Independent
accounting excludes network coding, shared representations, and reusable
intermediate results. Fixed weights exclude task redesign. The model ignores
latency, variance, failure, incentives, population dynamics, and ecological
fitness. It provides no evidence about actual AI systems, organisms,
conservation outcomes, energy use, or market value.
