\begin{abstract}
We study a deliberately narrow stewardship problem: finitely many independent
knowledge items forget at Poisson rates $\lambda_i>0$, are completely restored
by Poisson refreshes at rates $r_i\geq0$, and consume weighted refresh budget
$\sum_i c_i r_i\leq B$. The stationary retained fraction is
$a_i=r_i/(\lambda_i+r_i)$. We prove that the maximum worst-item retention is
$B/(B+\sum_i c_i\lambda_i)$ and, for $B>0$, is attained uniquely by allocating
refresh rate in proportion to forgetting hazard. The result is an elementary
stationary-flow and max-min resource-allocation certificate with close freshness,
reliability, spaced-practice, and continual-learning prior art. Novelty and
significance are unassessed. Fifteen preregistered numerical and symbolic checks
support the algebra but do not constitute a formal proof or empirical validation.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} knowledge stewardship; retention floor; max-min allocation; hazard-proportional refresh; continual learning

\section{Introduction}
Knowledge stewardship often asks a different question from average accuracy:
what maintenance policy keeps the most neglected item from becoming arbitrarily
stale? Continual-learning research shows that general optimal learning is hard
and can demand perfect memory \cite{knoblauch2020}; practical rehearsal policies
therefore use selective approximations \cite{harun2025}. We isolate a much
simpler, analytically solvable case in order to make the assumptions and the
worst-item guarantee explicit.

The contribution is a small certificate, not a general theory of learning. In
an independent two-state model, the weighted budget needed to guarantee a common
stationary retention floor $q$ is at least
$q(1-q)^{-1}\sum_i c_i\lambda_i$. This lower bound is tight. Its equality
condition yields a unique hazard-proportional allocation and a nonvacuous audit
rule: if estimated forgetting hazards double, their refresh rates must double to
preserve the same max-min optimum.

\section{Related prior work}
The mathematical core is close to classical availability and reliability
allocation. Age-of-information work studies scheduling and updating multiple
sources with richer queueing and freshness objectives \cite{yates2019}. Human
spacing experiments show that retention depends on timing, not merely on an
average review rate \cite{cepeda2008}. Continual-learning rehearsal studies
optimize which examples to revisit and how often \cite{harun2025}; more recent
theory demonstrates that replay can even increase forgetting under unfavorable
task geometry \cite{mahdaviyeh2026}. Those results make the independence and
monotone-restoration assumptions here substantive restrictions. We found no
basis to claim that the algebraic specialization itself is novel.

\section{Model and assumptions}
There are $n\geq1$ items. Item $i$ alternates between retained and unavailable.
Forgetting is a memoryless transition from retained to unavailable with rate
$\lambda_i>0$; refresh is a memoryless transition back with rate $r_i\geq0$.
One unit of rate costs $c_i>0$, and the steward has budget $B\geq0$:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{i=1}^n c_i r_i \leq B. \label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
Events are independent across items. Refresh is complete and cannot harm any
other item. There are no queues, setup delays, batches, shared examples, or
unknown hazards. The objective is stationary max-min availability, not mean
accuracy, age, human learning, recency, utility, or fairness across people.

Stationary flow balance gives $(1-a_i)r_i=a_i\lambda_i$, hence
\begin{equation}
  a_i=\frac{r_i}{\lambda_i+r_i}. \label{eq:availability}
\end{equation}

\section{Main results}
Let $L=\sum_i c_i\lambda_i>0$.

\textbf{Theorem 1 (retention-floor upper bound).}
For every feasible allocation,
\begin{equation}
  \min_i a_i \leq q^*:=\frac{B}{B+L}. \label{eq:floor}
\end{equation}

\emph{Proof.} Suppose all $a_i\geq q$ for $0\leq q<1$. Rearranging
Eq.~\eqref{eq:availability} gives
$r_i\geq q\lambda_i/(1-q)$. Multiplication by $c_i$ and summation imply
$B\geq qL/(1-q)$, or $q\leq B/(B+L)$. Taking
$q=\min_i a_i$ proves the claim. \hfill$\square$

\textbf{Theorem 2 (attainment and uniqueness).}
If $B>0$, the unique allocation attaining Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor} is
\begin{equation}
  r_i^*=\frac{B\lambda_i}{L}. \label{eq:allocation}
\end{equation}
It exhausts the budget and equalizes every item at $a_i=q^*$.

\emph{Proof.} Substitution gives
$a_i=(B\lambda_i/L)/(B\lambda_i/L+\lambda_i)=B/(B+L)$ and
$\sum_i c_i r_i^*=B$. Conversely, attainment requires every lower bound
$r_i\geq q^*\lambda_i/(1-q^*)=B\lambda_i/L$. Their weighted sum already
equals $B$, so no coordinate can be strictly larger. \hfill$\square$

For $B=0$, feasibility forces all rates and retentions to zero. As a nonvacuous
witness, take $B=10$, $(\lambda_1,\lambda_2)=(1,9)$, and unit costs. Then
$(r_1^*,r_2^*)=(1,9)$ and both retention fractions equal $1/2$.

\textbf{Objective boundary.} Maximizing the mean retention need not protect the
floor. With $B=1$, unit costs, and hazards $(0.1,1)$, a rate allocation concentrated
on the easy item can raise the mean while leaving the hard item at zero; the
max-min certificate instead assigns rates in the ratio $1{:}10$.

\section{Falsifiable predictions and tests}
Within the declared process, four observations would falsify the certificate:
(i) a feasible allocation exceeds $q^*$; (ii) a distinct positive-budget
allocation attains $q^*$; (iii) stationary occupancy fails to approach
$r_i/(r_i+\lambda_i)$; or (iv) hazard-proportional rates fail to equalize
retention. An empirical extension should preregister hazard estimation, compare
hazard-proportional, uniform, mean-optimized, and learned schedulers, and report
worst-task retention with uncertainty. Evidence of harmful replay or task
coupling invalidates this model rather than confirming it.

\section{Discussion and conclusion}
The certificate converts a worst-item stewardship goal into a simple policy:
spend refresh capacity in proportion to estimated forgetting hazard, independent
of cost except through the common normalization. Higher-cost hazards depress the
achievable floor because they increase $L$, while relative rates remain determined
by hazards. This is useful as an audit baseline: deviations may be justified by
utility, uncertainty, queues, or coupling, but those reasons must be named rather
than hidden inside an average score.

The correct route is significance review in the working corpus. The result does
not warrant Canon or spine admission, a product wrapper, or a claim of general
continual-learning optimality.

\section{Limitations}
Poisson events exclude deterministic spacing and schedule history. Complete
refresh excludes partial learning, saturation, consolidation, and interference.
Known constant hazards exclude estimation error and nonstationarity. Independence
excludes shared examples and representation geometry; replay can be actively
harmful in such settings \cite{mahdaviyeh2026}. Stationary availability ignores
transient risk and age. Max-min retention is not a normative fairness guarantee.
No human participant, model-training, customer, energy, ecological, or revenue
evidence was collected.
