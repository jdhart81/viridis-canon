\begin{abstract}
Autonomous applications often share a finite human-review reserve. We isolate a
two-stream linear ledger in which reviewer time removes declared expected loss
at supplied rates $r_1\ge r_2$. Moving any feasible increment $\delta\ge0$ of
review time from stream 2 to stream 1 changes review benefit by exactly
$\delta(r_1-r_2)\ge0$, preserves total reviewer time, and cannot increase
residual loss. Equal rates make every allocation with the same total time
equivalent. This is the elementary exchange argument behind fractional
knapsack allocation, recorded as an auditable baseline rather than a new
learning-to-defer algorithm. It does not estimate reviewer value, calibrate
risk, or validate autonomous deployment.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} learning to defer; human oversight; review capacity; autonomous agents; risk allocation

\section{Introduction and prior work}
Learning-to-defer systems decide whether a model or a human expert should make
a prediction. Mozannar et al. show that even linear classifier--rejector pairs
lead to nontrivial optimization and NP-hardness, while giving an exact MILP and
a realizable-consistent surrogate \cite{mozannar2023}. Tailor et al. adapt
deferral to previously unseen experts with meta-learning \cite{tailor2024}.
Zhang et al. treat multiple experts under an explicit AI-only coverage
constraint \cite{zhang2024}. Montreuil et al. extend the setting to online data
and dynamically varying expert pools with regret guarantees
\cite{montreuil2026}.

The local Viridis Autonomous Agent Collective specification says that agents
execute while humans direct, and reserves human authority for high-stakes
decisions and disputes. A finite review reserve is therefore a real design
constraint. This note asks a deliberately smaller question than the cited
work: after a system supplies fixed loss-reduction rates, what allocation is
implied by a linear two-stream ledger? The answer is established
continuous-knapsack bookkeeping, so the result is classified as a methods
note.

\section{Model and assumptions}
Let $t_1,t_2\ge0$ be reviewer time assigned to two splittable task streams and
let $r_1,r_2\ge0$ be supplied reductions in declared expected loss per unit of
reviewer time. Define review benefit and residual loss by
\begin{align}
B(t_1,t_2)&=r_1t_1+r_2t_2,\\
L(t_1,t_2)&=L_0-B(t_1,t_2).
\end{align}
The ledger assumes compatible units, constant marginal rates, divisible work,
additive effects, and fungible reviewer time. It does not infer the rates from
confidence, disagreement, model scores, or historical outcomes.

\section{Results}
\textbf{Proposition 1 (exchange identity).} Moving a feasible amount
$\delta$ from stream 2 to stream 1 yields
\begin{equation}
B(t_1+\delta,t_2-\delta)-B(t_1,t_2)
=\delta(r_1-r_2). \label{eq:exchange}
\end{equation}
The transfer preserves total time. If $r_1\ge r_2$ and $\delta\ge0$, benefit
cannot fall and residual loss cannot rise.

\textbf{Proposition 2 (two-stream ordering).} For a fixed reserve
$t_1+t_2=T$, if $r_1\ge r_2$ then
\begin{equation}
B(t_1,t_2)\le r_1T.
\end{equation}
Thus stream 1 is filled first whenever its available workload can absorb the
reserve. If $r_1=r_2$, every feasible split has benefit $r_1T$; the allocation
is not identifiable from this objective.

\textbf{Witnesses.} At $(r_1,r_2,T)=(4,2,3)$, all review on stream 1 removes
12 declared loss units. From $L_0=20$, residual loss is 8. Splitting the same
reserve as $(2,1)$ removes only 10. Reversing the rates while retaining the
same priority is an exact negative control: three units on rate 2 remove less
loss than three units on rate 4.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
Equation~\ref{eq:exchange} is useful mainly because it exposes what a
production system would have to justify. The rates must be calibrated against
a declared harm function, uncertainty must be carried into prioritization,
and review must have a causal effect rather than merely select easier cases.
Queueing delay, expert availability, fatigue, nonlinear marginal returns,
correlated model--reviewer errors, subgroup harms, indivisible cases,
distribution shift, strategic behavior, and feedback from interventions can
all invalidate the simple ordering.

The note does not establish that a model knows when it is wrong, that human
review is better, or that maximum expected loss reduction is an ethically
sufficient objective. It grants no authority for outreach, finance, disputes,
rights, or other high-stakes actions. No calibrated input schema, validated
decision family, empirical loss model, or operating/refusal envelope exists,
so there is no ViridisOS runtime kernel. The result is not a Canon-spine
candidate and carries no publication, adoption, delivery, or revenue claim.
