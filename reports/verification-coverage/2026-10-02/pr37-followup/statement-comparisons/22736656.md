\begin{abstract}
Suppose a declared corridor service requires two compatible directional
movement streams with supplied capacities $x,y\ge0$.  If reciprocal service is
defined as $m=\min\{x,y\}$, unit costs are $c,d\ge0$, and
$cx+dy\le B$, then $(c+d)m\le B$.  The deficit from equality separates exactly
into unused budget and a cost-weighted imbalance term.  With positive costs and
full spend, equality holds exactly when the two capacities are balanced.  This
is elementary conditional bookkeeping and is classified as a methods note. It
does not estimate movement, mutualism, corridor performance, or ecological
outcomes.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} habitat corridor; reciprocal movement;
multispecies connectivity; bottleneck; budget; symbiosis

\section{Introduction}
Corridor planning increasingly treats multiple species, interaction networks,
and movement-mediated ecological functions rather than one isolated path.
Budget-constrained multispecies design can realize economies of scale
\cite{dilkina}; empirical seed-dispersal networks show that mobile species link
habitats in spatial multilayer systems \cite{timoteo,emer}. These results are
far richer than a two-number ledger, but they motivate a basic audit question:
if a claimed service needs movement in both directions, how much of two supplied
capacities is jointly usable?

Earlier Viridis runs studied nested mutualism, coupled sensing and corridors,
shared-edge reliability, and affine two-route stewardship. Here we isolate a
different object: a stipulated reciprocal minimum and the exact budget deficit
created by imbalance.

\section{Model and assumptions}
Let $x$ and $y$ be supplied nonnegative capacities in compatible service units.
Define reciprocal service by
\[
m(x,y)=\min\{x,y\}.
\]
Let $c,d\ge0$ be supplied unit costs and let the linear spend be
$S=cx+dy\le B$. The minimum is a declared decision rule: it represents a
service that fails to increase when only the already larger directional stream
grows. It is not derived from demography, genetics, movement ecology, or
mutualistic-network dynamics.

\section{Main result}
Because $m\le x$ and $m\le y$, nonnegative costs give
\[
cm\le cx,\qquad dm\le dy.
\]
Summing and applying the budget yields
\begin{equation}
(c+d)m\le cx+dy\le B.\label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
The deficit has an exact interpretation. If $x\le y$, then
\begin{equation}
B-(c+d)m=(B-S)+d(y-x).\label{eq:left}
\end{equation}
If $y\le x$, then
\begin{equation}
B-(c+d)m=(B-S)+c(x-y).\label{eq:right}
\end{equation}
Thus the deficit is unused budget plus the cost paid for capacity above the
reciprocal bottleneck. When $c,d>0$ and $S=B$, equality in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:bound} holds if and only if $x=y$. For example,
$(c,d,x,y,B)=(2,3,5,5,25)$ is a positive equality witness, whereas
$(2,3,2,5,25)$ has reciprocal service $2$ and deficit $15$.

\section{Discussion}
The decomposition distinguishes two different reasons for missing the bound:
resources can remain unused, or resources can fund capacity that does not raise
the stipulated reciprocal minimum. Reporting these quantities can expose a
model mismatch before a more consequential spatial optimization is run.

Balance is not a universal conservation prescription. One direction may serve
different species, seasons, or functions; excess capacity may provide
redundancy, recolonization, climate tracking, or option value. Those benefits
must enter a richer objective rather than being silently discarded.
