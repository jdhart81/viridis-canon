\begin{abstract}
Suppose a supplied raw capacity $C\ge0$ passes through two sequential screens
with declared retention fractions $a,b\in[0,1]$. If retained capacity is
$R=Cab$, then the total loss decomposes exactly as
$C-R=C(1-a)+Ca(1-b)$. Final retention is invariant to reversing the two
factors, while stage-level loss attribution generally is not. This elementary
identity is a methods note, not an empirical model of alignment, oversight, or
intelligence. The factors require external calibration and the model excludes
correlation, adaptation, and strategic behavior.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} intelligence capacity; alignment; scalable
oversight; serial composition; retention; assurance

\section{Introduction}
Alignment verification and scalable oversight ask how limited evaluators can
test or supervise more capable systems. Brown et al. formalize efficient value
alignment tests \cite{brown}; Burns et al. study weak-to-strong supervision
\cite{burns}; and Kenton et al. compare debate, consultancy, and direct
judgment across several tasks \cite{kenton}. Goel et al. show that correlated
model failures can weaken oversight methods \cite{goel}. These works make
clear that assurance effectiveness is empirical and context dependent.

This note does not propose an oversight protocol. It isolates a small ledger
identity for situations where two retention factors have already been supplied
and a user wants to account for compounded capacity without double-counting
losses.

\section{Related work and novelty boundary}
The cited studies treat test complexity, supervision gaps, protocol comparison,

\section{Model and assumptions}
Let $C\ge0$ be a supplied raw capacity in one compatible unit. Let
$a,b\in[0,1]$ be supplied stage-retention fractions. Define
\[
R(C,a,b)=Cab,
\quad L_1(C,a)=C(1-a),
\quad L_2(C,a,b)=Ca(1-b).
\]
The second factor applies to the first stage's output. The model assumes fixed
fractions for a declared context. It does not infer them from model confidence,
judge accuracy, debate outcomes, or any cited study.

\section{Main result}
Nonnegativity gives $R\ge0$. Because $b\le1$,
$R\le Ca$; because $a\le1$, $R\le C$. Direct expansion yields
\begin{equation}
C-R=C(1-a)+Ca(1-b)=L_1+L_2.\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
The same final retention occurs after reversing the factors because
$Cab=Cba$. Consequently the total loss is also invariant, but attribution is
not: the first stage is charged on the raw capacity and the second only on the
survivor.

At $C=12$, $a=3/4$, and $b=2/3$, retained capacity is $6$ and each stage is
assigned a loss of $3$. Reversing the factors preserves the final $6$ but
assigns losses $4$ and $2$.

\section{Discussion}
The ledger prevents a common arithmetic error: charging the second-stage loss
against the raw capacity after the first stage has already removed part of it.
It also separates an order-invariant total from order-dependent attribution.

The identity does not say that two real screens are independent, stable, or
well calibrated. If the second-stage behavior depends on which cases survive
the first, one scalar $b$ may change with stage order. Correlated failure,
adaptive agents, evaluator incentives, task distribution, and uncertainty
must be modeled directly rather than hidden inside this note.
