\begin{abstract}
Suppose two intervention stages receive supplied nonnegative work quantities
$w_1,w_2$ in one commensurable unit, with externally supplied useful-work
fractions $e_1,e_2\in[0,1]$. Define useful work
$U=e_1w_1+e_2w_2$ and a modeled dissipative remainder
$D=(1-e_1)w_1+(1-e_2)w_2$. Then $U+D=w_1+w_2$, and both terms lie between zero
and total supplied work. If both efficiencies are at most $e_{\max}$, then
$U\le e_{\max}(w_1+w_2)$; when $e_{\max}>0$, achieving a declared target
$T\le U$ requires total supplied work at least $T/e_{\max}$. These elementary
identities are a methods note, not measurements of entropy production, exergy
destruction, ecosystem recovery, or planetary habitability.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} ecopoiesis; ecological restoration; work ledger;
energy accounting; efficiency; staged intervention

\section{Introduction}
Ecological restoration changes energy flow, function, and material cycling in
ways that depend on system history and context. Wang et al. measured changing
soil-food-web energy flux across forest recovery stages \cite{wang}. Bega et
al. found that riparian restoration effects on production, respiration, and
nutrient uptake depended on environmental context \cite{bega}. Shang et al.
reported taxon-specific hysteresis under nutrient loading \cite{shang}.
Transparent accounting is useful, but empirical energy flow, ecological
function, financial cost, and intervention work are not interchangeable.

This note isolates a minimal bookkeeping rule for a future ecopoiesis or
restoration design that already has a defined system boundary, a common work
unit, and calibrated stage fractions. It does not supply those measurements.

\section{Related work and novelty boundary}
Andres et al. match restoration implementation costs to outcomes across methods

\section{Model and assumptions}
Let $w_1,w_2\ge0$ be supplied work quantities in the same unit. Let
$e_1,e_2\in[0,1]$ be supplied useful-work fractions for a declared boundary and
time window. Define
\[
W=w_1+w_2,\qquad U=e_1w_1+e_2w_2,
\]
\[
D=(1-e_1)w_1+(1-e_2)w_2.
\]
The word ``dissipated'' labels the accounting remainder only. It does not
establish heat generation, entropy production, exergy destruction, ecological
damage, or recoverability. The model assumes additive stages with no coupling,
storage, recovered waste, feedback, hysteresis, or changing boundary.

\section{Main result}
Nonnegative inputs and fractions give $U\ge0$; fractions no greater than one
give $D\ge0$. Direct expansion yields
\begin{equation}
U+D=e_1w_1+(1-e_1)w_1+e_2w_2+(1-e_2)w_2=W.\label{eq:partition}
\end{equation}
Therefore $0\le U\le W$ and $0\le D\le W$. If
$e_1,e_2\le e_{\max}$, then
\begin{equation}
U\le e_{\max}w_1+e_{\max}w_2=e_{\max}W.\label{eq:ceiling}
\end{equation}
For $e_{\max}>0$ and a target $T\le U$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:ceiling} gives the
necessary floor $W\ge T/e_{\max}$. This is not a sufficiency claim.

At $w_1=8$, $w_2=4$, $e_1=3/4$, and $e_2=1/2$, the ledger gives $U=8$,
$D=4$, and $W=12$.

\section{Discussion}
The ledger forces three questions before optimization: what crosses the system
boundary, which work unit is common, and what observation justifies each useful
fraction? It also prevents a target from being presented without the necessary
work implied by a declared efficiency ceiling.

Nothing here says that more supplied work improves an ecosystem, that the two
stages are independent, or that a remainder is ecologically harmless. Field
systems can store energy, recycle material, cross thresholds, exhibit
hysteresis, and respond asynchronously. Those behaviors must be measured and
modeled rather than hidden inside fixed fractions.
